# Writing

## Baseline

- **Authority** — Preserve meaning, facts, evidence, certainty, attribution, citations, terminology, protected material,
  and required structure unless the task explicitly changes them.
- **Originality** — Derive expression from the actual purpose, evidence, audience, genre, author, and context rather than
  generic templates or habitual framing.
- **Structure** — For broad revision, work from purpose and scope through document structure, paragraph relationships,
  sentence relationships, then wording. Keep a focused edit local when those larger surfaces are not in scope.
- **Expression** — Prefer exact, natural wording and stable terminology. Remove throat-clearing, duplicated meaning,
  ornamental qualification, and generic transitions only when they do no real work.
- **Voice** — Preserve supplied voice, defensible cadence, deliberate repetition, and functional formality. Do not make
  prose uniformly plain merely for consistency.
- **Claims** — Never invent specificity, examples, anecdotes, quotations, reactions, authority, or evidence to make prose
  stronger, more concrete, or more human.
- **Validation** — Re-read changed prose for lost obligations, unsupported additions, changed claim strength, broken
  logic, unstable terminology, flattened voice, and format damage.

## Language

languages, apply the baseline and the best available project or publication guidance.

## Technical English

When `simple-english` is available, use it for substantive technical documentation, procedures, instructions, runbooks,
error messages, API guides, and other operational prose where ASD-STE100 controlled-language constraints are useful.
Do not duplicate its rules here.

For scholarly, scientific, and other authored technical prose, use STE-derived clarity principles selectively: prefer
stable terminology, direct verbs, clear noun compounds, grammatical parallelism, and low wordiness. Do not impose hard
sentence limits, modal restrictions, punctuation bans, or other controlled-language rules when they would alter
scientific uncertainty, disciplinary meaning, authorial voice, or genre convention. Full STE remains available when the
user or governing publication explicitly requires it.
